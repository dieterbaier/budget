[[adr-022-ubiquitous-language]]
== ADR-022: A Term Register For The Domain Language

=== Status

Accepted. Decided by the owner on 2026-07-31, after he observed that the domain
is spoken in German while the code is English and that nothing in this repository
had ever decided that. Reworked on 2026-08-01 after his review, and corrected on
2026-08-02 when applying it found a factual claim in it to be false; see
<<adr-022-what-the-review-changed,What the review changed>>.

=== Decision

The project has one ubiquitous language, and it is **recorded rather than
assumed**. Every domain concept has one entry in the glossary, and that entry
fixes both forms the concept is allowed to take: the term the domain expert uses
when he speaks, and the identifier used in the domain model and in the
architecture documentation. Usually they are the same word. Where they are not,
the difference is a translation the expert has examined and accepted, and it is
legible in the register.

Four rules follow.

. *The identifier is chosen per concept, not per language.* Where an English
  rendering carries the concept faithfully, it is the identifier — `Category` for
  "Kategorie". Where it loses meaning, the expert's German word is the
  identifier, spelled as an identifier (`Verwendungszweck`, `Sollansparung`), not
  translated and not glossed.
. *Faithfulness is judged once, by the domain expert, when the concept enters the
  register.* It is not re-judged at each use, and it is not judged by whoever
  happens to be writing the code. A concept that is in the register is settled.
. *The glossary is the register.* No type in `domain/model/` and no term in the
  architecture documentation may name a concept that has no entry; no entry may
  name a concept the domain does not have; and no identifier for a domain concept
  may differ from the one its entry records.
. *An invented concept is adopted, not translated.* Some concepts came from the
  architecture rather than from the expert — the tracking window, the cash
  runway, the overspending signal. These have no German original. They enter the
  language when the expert accepts a term for them, which is a different act from
  translating one.

*Rule 3 is staged, and the staging ends.* From the day the register exists it
binds the architecture documentation and every domain type added or changed
after it. Identifiers implemented before the register are not retroactively in
violation: they are named in the glossary and renamed under issue #79, and the
rule is unstaged when that issue closes. Until then a reader who finds
`FixedCost` in `domain/model/` is looking at a known exception with an owner,
not at a rule being ignored.

Staging it is the weaker choice and is taken deliberately. The alternative was
to rename before the register could be called binding, which reaches an executed
Flyway migration and a question — whether this project's migration history may
be rewritten — that is not a language question and should not be answered in a
hurry to make a rule true.

What these rules forbid is a translation nobody agreed to and nobody can see —
the kind a developer performs at the keyboard, in the second it takes to pick an
English word. They do not forbid translation. That is a weaker rule than DDD's
own, deliberately, and the price is stated under
<<adr-022-consequences-negative,Negative>>.

Nothing here governs how the owner speaks. He says "Kategorie"; the register
records that the identifier for that concept is `Category`, and the two are the
same concept because the register says so.

Technical vocabulary is unaffected. `repository`, `service`, `findAll`, `Money`
and the framework's own words stay English; the rule governs the nouns that name
*domain concepts*.

=== Context

DDD's ubiquitous-language rule is not about which natural language a team uses.
It is about there being **only one**, with no translation step between what the
expert says and what the code says, because a translation step is where knowledge
leaks.

This decision does not remove that step. For every concept whose identifier is an
English rendering it keeps one, and pays for it by making it explicit: examined
once by the expert, written into the register, and checkable afterwards. The
strict reading — no step anywhere — is the second option below, and it was
rejected on cost rather than on merit.

The leak has already cost this project once, before the rule was written down.
`FixedCost.anchorDate` named a *fixed reference point*. The domain has no such
thing: it has "letzte Zahlung", a value that moves forward every time the cost is
paid, and from which the next due date and the reserve are derived. The English
name invented a concept, and it stood in the model, the schema and the glossary
for four days without anyone reading it twice. Had the field been called
`letzteZahlung`, the error would have been impossible to make.

Two more of the same kind were queued when this decision was taken.
`Sollansparung` had been rendered as `reserve requirement`: the German says *what
should have been saved up by now*, the English says *what must be held*. Those
are different quantities that happen to be equal today.
`Verwendungszweck` had been rendered as `Purpose`, which is generic where the
original is the banking term for what a payment is for.

Neither had been marked as a translation. That is the actual failure mode — not
English, but *unagreed* translation. `Category` for "Kategorie" costs nothing and
nobody would object to it.

Two of the three lossy terms were still unimplemented when this was decided —
`Sollansparung` in issue #16 and `Verwendungszweck` in #58. The third was not.
`letzte Zahlung` was already in the code as `anchorDate`, which is the example
this decision is built on, and renaming it reaches the domain record, the JPA
entity, the persistence adapter and a Flyway migration that has already run. See
<<adr-022-what-the-review-changed,What the review changed>>.

=== Considered Options

==== A register, with the identifier chosen per concept (chosen)

Faithful renderings stay English; lossy ones keep the expert's word. Each entry
records both forms, so the mapping between them is a reviewed artifact rather
than something a reader reconstructs. A check enforces it.

==== Domain words everywhere, English only for plumbing

Every domain noun in the expert's language, through the model, the API payload
and the client types. Structurally the strongest answer: with no translation
boundary anywhere, the `anchorDate` class of error cannot occur at all.

Rejected on cost against benefit. It rewrites the model, the schema, the REST
payload, the TypeScript types and the Zod schemas, to protect a set of terms that
is small and already identified — and the concepts where translation actually
hurts are the minority. Worth revisiting only if this project ever gains
German-speaking contributors, which a single-user product will not.

==== Declare the domain language to be English

Fix the identifier as English for every concept, by policy, and keep the code
untouched.

Rejected because it decides the rendering before the concept has been looked at.
Where no faithful English word exists the policy still requires one:
`Sollansparung` becomes `reserve requirement`, which is a different quantity, and
`Barabhebung` has no English word at all and would need an exception on its first
day. It is the chosen option with rule 1 deleted and rule 2 answered in advance —
which is the mechanism that produced `anchorDate`, promoted to a policy.

Enforceability is not a row. All three options are checkable against a register,
so the criterion separates nothing; it is a requirement each option had to meet
rather than a way of telling them apart.

The chosen option and the rejected first alternative are close, and the matrix
says so. The deciding criterion was cost today, not correctness: option two is
the better model and the worse trade.

=== Consequences

==== Positive

* The class of error that produced `anchorDate` has to get past the one person
  who would notice it. A name that invents a concept can still be proposed, but
  it cannot be adopted at the keyboard: it reaches the register first, and the
  register is where the expert reads it.
* The decision was cheap in implemented code, though not free: two of the three
  lossy terms were still in the backlog when it was taken, and the third —
  `anchorDate` — was not.
* The glossary stops being merely descriptive and becomes authoritative. It is
  the artifact a later reader consults to find out what something is *called*,
  not only what it means.

[[adr-022-consequences-negative]]
==== Negative

* A translation step survives, between what the expert says and what the code
  says, wherever the identifier is an English rendering. This ADR makes it
  explicit and reviewable; it does not abolish it, and the claim that this
  project translates nowhere would be false and is not made. Abolishing it is the
  second option, and it was priced and declined.
* Identifiers will be mixed-language. `record Fixkosten` next to
  `FixedCostRepository` is not going to look tidy, and no amount of rule-writing
  makes it look tidy.
* "Is this rendering faithful?" is a judgement, and this project's own record is
  a long list of unchecked judgements drifting. The mitigation is real but
  partial: the judgement is made once per term and written down, so it is not
  re-made silently — but nothing validates the judgement itself.
* The glossary now has three jobs: explaining a concept, recording the term the
  expert speaks, and registering the identifier that term maps to — with a
  display form arriving from ADR-023 on top. Splitting them into separate
  artifacts was considered and rejected because it costs a second lookup and
  invites the copies to disagree; the cost of not splitting is that one table
  serves several readers at once.

==== Neutral or Follow-Up

* This decision does not settle the UI language. A mixed-language term list would
  produce an interface reading "Category", "Sollansparung" and "Fixed costs" side
  by side, which is not a language. See
  xref:adr-023-ui-language[ADR-023].
* The term list itself is not in this ADR. Building it needs a working session
  with the domain expert, and a first pass already found questions no rule
  answers: the expert says "Rentengap", a German-English hybrid; he uses
  "Erstattung" and "Gutschrift" for what appears to be one concept; and
  "Vermögen" is his word for the total where the glossary currently calls the
  total `Net worth` and a single position `Asset`.
* The check in rule 3 is not implemented. Until it is, rule 3 is a convention,
  and this project has ten recorded instances of what happens to those.
* ADR-009's aggregate map still described `FixedCost` as carrying an "anchor
  date"; it is corrected in the same change as this ADR.

[[adr-022-what-the-review-changed]]
=== What The Review Changed

The first version of this ADR required the agreed term to be used unchanged "in
the domain model, in the architecture documentation and in conversation", while
also accepting `Category` as the term for "Kategorie". Read together, those two
statements obliged a German-speaking domain expert to say "Category" — the exact
consequence this ADR gives as its reason for rejecting *Domain declared English*.
The rule was therefore unsatisfiable as written, and the option comparison rested
on a criterion the chosen option did not meet either.

The owner named the resolution in his review: a binding register with an
explicit, expert-checked mapping between the spoken term and the identifier. It
permits translation and forbids the silent or lossy kind, which is the failure
this ADR had already identified as the real one. The Decision, rule 1, the
rejected option and the matrix were reworked to say that; the position on which
identifier belongs to which concept did not change.

Recorded here rather than fixed silently, because the contradiction is the more
useful half: two paragraphs of an ADR can each be defensible and jointly
impossible, and only reading them against one another finds it.

==== The claim that the decision cost no implemented code

It did cost some, and this ADR said otherwise twice — in Context and in the first
list of positive consequences. Both are corrected above.

`letzte Zahlung` was listed with `Sollansparung` and `Verwendungszweck` as a term
still in the backlog. It was not. It was in the code as `anchorDate`, in the
domain record, the JPA entity, the persistence adapter and a Flyway migration
that had already run — and `anchorDate` is the example this whole decision is
built on, named in its Context and in its first positive consequence.

Nobody checked before the ADR merged. It was found four days later, by writing
`progress/domain-model.adoc`, because that format asks for the *order* of the
work rather than a list of it: writing down that the rename has to happen before
the check that enforces the agreed terms is what made someone look at where the
term actually sat.

Two things about the error are worth keeping. It is not the through-line — no
check went green over it, because prose has no checks. And it is not the
unsatisfiable-rule shape above either: both statements were internally
consistent and simply untrue, which is the one failure a careful re-reading of
the document cannot catch. Only the repository could, and only if someone asked
it.

The decision itself is unaffected. What changed is the claim about what the
decision cost.

==== Rule 3 was stated as if it already held

The review of the register pass found that merging it would have put the
repository in immediate violation of this ADR. Rule 3 binds `domain/model/`, and
`FixedCost`, `PaymentInterval`, `anchorDate` and `monthlyPortion` were still in
the code — so the rule would have been false on the day it was declared
enforceable.

Two ways out were available: rename first, or say when the rule takes effect. The
rename reaches 22 files and an executed Flyway migration, and it depends on a
question this ADR has no business answering — whether a migration history with
one deployment may be rewritten. Deciding that quickly, in order to make a
sentence true, is the failure mode this document exists to name.

So rule 3 is now staged, with the exception listed in the register and an issue
that ends the staging. That is weaker than the original wording and it is honest,
where the original was strong and false. The Decision records the transition
rather than leaving a reader to discover the gap.

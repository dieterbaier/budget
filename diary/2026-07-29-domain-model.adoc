== Day 5 — 29 July 2026: the model was a level short

No code changed today. The owner put five screenshots of the Notion budget this
application replaces into `.private/`, and the job was to map them onto what the
documentation claims and what the backend actually does.

That turned out to be the most productive day so far, which is worth noticing on
its own: four days of building produced a model that a twenty-minute look at the
real data corrected in three places.

=== The first commit was a `.gitignore` line

`.private/` was not ignored. The screenshots hold real balances, real payees and
a real pension figure, and this repository is public. One `git add -A` away.

Recorded here because the sequencing is the lesson: the material arrived, was
read, and only then did anyone check whether it could leak. The safe order is the
other one.

=== The figure that was answering a different question

The sharpest finding, and it belongs in the through-line.

`MonthlyExpenditureCalculator` sums the EXPENSE transactions of the current
calendar month. Increment 1 was built around it, it is covered by tests, ArchUnit
passes, coverage passes, the requirements overview describes it, and the roadmap
calls it "the single view the owner would miss most".

The number the owner actually reads is a *long-run average*: every recorded
booking divided by the months tracked so far. It answers "what does this cost me
per month", not "what have I spent since the first".

Both figures are legitimate. Only one of them is the product. Nothing in the
pipeline could have noticed, because everything was verifying that the
implementation matched the specification — and the specification was wrong.

The Notion divisor gave it away: 15.63. Three separate rows all divided by the
same non-integer. A calendar month cannot produce that.

This is a fourth way for a green build to be quiet, and the most expensive one.
The three the diary already had — wrong wiring, wrong threshold, wrong belief —
are all recoverable by testing harder. This one is not reachable by any amount of
testing discipline, because the tests were right about everything they were asked.

=== A name that hid a moving value

`FixedCost.anchorDate`, described in the glossary as "an anchor date", modelled as
a fixed reference point.

It is the date of the *last payment*. It moves forward every time the cost is
paid, and it is the input to two derived figures. "Anchor" is precisely the wrong
word for something that advances — and the name had been sitting in the domain,
the schema and the glossary for four days without anyone reading it twice.

Nothing was broken. `monthlyPortion()` never touched the field. The name was
wrong in a place where being wrong had no consequence *yet*, which is exactly how
it survives until it does.

=== Two derivations recovered, one constant invented

The screenshots show computed columns without their formulas. Three were
reverse-engineered from the numbers.

Two held up and were confirmed by the owner, and they fully specify two open
issues that had been vague for a week:

reserve requirement (#16):: whole months since the last payment × the monthly
portion, zero for monthly costs. Two independent rows both landed on 4 × 5.00 =
20.00.

cash runway (#17):: the monthly figure ÷ 30, giving a daily rate. Three rows
agreed on the divisor.

The third did not, and the failure is the part worth keeping. Assuming
`minimum need = costs at retirement − expected pension` left a residual of
*173.50* in both pension scenarios. Identical in both. So it was reported as a
constant to be explained — tax on the pension, health insurance, something.

The owner's reply: *"Woher hast Du die konstanten 173,50? Ich finde diese Zahl
nirgends."*

It is not in his data. It is the gap between his numbers and *my assumed
formula*, and it appeared in both rows because both rows used the same wrong
assumption. A residual is not a discovered quantity. It looked like a finding
because it was stable, and stability is what a systematic error looks like from
the inside.

The pension formula is now specified from scratch in #18, with an explicit
instruction not to reconstruct it from the sheet — the owner reports a known
calculation error in that part of it, so reverse-engineering would have imported
the bug along with the arithmetic.

=== Asking about the model and getting an answer about the workflow

The Notion budget has a level the domain model does not: `Verwendungszweck`,
below the category. `Fernwärme` under `Nebenkosten`, `Streaming (Musik)` under
`Lizenzen`.

The question put to the owner was whether a purpose belongs to one category or
crosses them. The answer that came back described *data entry* — "the purpose is
entered at the transaction, so it is automatically visible for all transactions
of a category" — which does not settle it. But the option he picked on the
follow-up question carried the invariant `purpose.category == category`, which
does.

The question was too abstract to answer. What resolved it was a concrete
scenario: *you book the vet and type "Vasko"; two weeks later you book dog food
and type "Vasko" — same purpose, or two that happen to share a name?* One
sentence, and the modelling question became answerable.

Worth generalising: when a modelling question is answered with a description of
the workflow, the question was about the wrong thing. Ask for a case, not for a
rule.

The single row in the real data that contradicts the hierarchy — a booking
tagged `Gesundheit / Arzt Dieter` with the purpose `Vasko`, next to a whole
`Hund` group — was almost certainly a mis-tag rather than a design intent. But it
was the only evidence either way, so it had to be put to the owner rather than
quietly ruled out.

=== A risk that was a missing feature

Fixed costs are deliberately never booked; their definition already contributes
its monthly portion. So a fixed-cost debit transcribed from the bank statement by
mistake is counted twice, and the application cannot detect it — the duplicate is
a well-formed booking in a plausible category.

That was raised as a risk to record and accept, and `R-002` was written.

The owner's answer reframed it. He already runs the control in Notion: a view of
the fixed costs currently due, reconciled by eye. And it does more than catch
duplicates — a cost whose last payment is older than its own interval is a
contract that no longer exists, like the cancelled gym membership still inflating
the monthly figure every month until someone notices.

So the mitigation was not a control to be designed. It was a feature already in
daily use that nobody had written down, and it became issue #61.

Worth keeping as a question to ask: *does the user already do this by hand?* If
yes, it is a requirement wearing a risk's clothing.

`R-002` still exists, reviewed and released — but with its mitigation named and
the trigger for going further recorded: if this causes *repeated* mistakes, a
duplicate-suspicion warning becomes worth building. Not a deferral; a condition.

=== What landed

Documentation only, six commits, PR #57.

* Q-PO-005 to Q-PO-010 record the six decisions; Q-PO-004 was wrong in three
  places and is corrected *in place* rather than rewritten, so the record still
  shows what the earlier notes claimed.
* The glossary gained `Purpose`, tracking window, last payment, due fixed cost,
  refunds per month and the inflation series — and several existing entries
  described something the owner does not do.
* One sentence from the session governs the rest and is now written where a later
  reader will find it: *this is not an accounting system.* It explains why there
  are no derived balances, no business-day handling and no automatic
  reconciliation. Those had looked like gaps.
* Five new issues (#58–#62), five revised (#10, #15, #16, #17, #18).

The backlog was the point as much as the documentation. Two issues that had said
"compute the reserve" and "compute the runway" now carry the arithmetic and a
worked example.

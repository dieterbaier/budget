== Day 7 — 1 August 2026: rules that could not be followed

No product code changed today. Two pull requests that had been open since
yesterday morning went through four rounds of review between them, and every
round found the same shape of defect: a rule that reads well and cannot be
obeyed.

=== Day 6 has no recap

31 July produced ADR-022, ADR-023 and the first version of the language-profile
skill — commits `f20842a` at 08:57 and `10088d6` at 09:18, pull requests #67 and
#71 opened within twenty minutes of each other. There is no `2026-07-31` file in
this directory.

It is worth recording as the first entry of a day whose whole subject is the
start and end of a working day. The day without the ritual is the day with no
record, and nobody noticed at the time. That is the argument for
`skills/clock-out/SKILL.md` in one sentence, and it is stronger evidence than
anything written in the skill itself.

The gap is not being backfilled. What happened on 31 July is in the two pull
requests and in the commits; what we *believed* on 31 July is gone, and inventing
it a day later would be exactly the failure the diary exists to prevent.

=== The rule that obliged the owner to speak English

ADR-022 fixed the ubiquitous language. Its central rule required the agreed term
to be used unchanged "in the domain model, in the architecture documentation and
in conversation", and in the same document it accepted `Category` as the term for
"Kategorie".

Read together, those two sentences oblige a German-speaking domain expert to say
"Category" out loud. And the ADR itself gives precisely that consequence as its
reason for rejecting the option *Domain declared English*: "the moment he says
'Sollansparung' in a working session, the rule is broken".

So the central rule was unsatisfiable, and the option comparison rested on a
criterion the chosen option failed too. Neither of us saw it while the ADR was
being written; the review found it by reading two paragraphs against one another.

The resolution the owner named is better than the original: a binding register
with an explicit, expert-checked mapping between the spoken term and the code
identifier. Translation is permitted; the silent or lossy kind is not — which is
the failure mode the ADR had already identified as the real one. The position on
which identifier belongs to which concept did not change at all. Only the claim
about what the decision achieves.

`37cbf85` reworked it, and the ADR now carries a *What The Review Changed*
section rather than being fixed quietly. The contradiction is the more useful
half: two paragraphs can each be defensible and jointly impossible.

=== The skill that broke its own boundary

The language-profile skill states, under *Purpose*, that it governs how a text
reads while structure, metadata and lifecycle stay with the toolkit. The first
row of its own table then said ADR, constraint, quality scenario and risk are
"superseded, never rewritten".

That is false for two of the four, and checkably so: the toolkit's
`skills/risk/SKILL.md` names updating an existing risk as a core workflow, and
`skills/quality-scenario/SKILL.md` reads the same. Both are listed under *Read
first* in the skill that contradicted them. Following the rule would have created
a new risk artifact per reassessment.

The second fault is the one to keep. "Never revised, only superseded" is not a
register rule at all — it is lifecycle governance. The skill was overriding the
area it declares it does not own, in the first row of its table, on the first day
it existed.

Two more rounds followed, both the same shape. Profile 2 grouped risk and quality
scenario correctly and then wrote only about *grades* — whether the current grade
is right, what would move it, how terse an assessment cell may be — while a
quality scenario has no grades at all; an agent applying it would have had to
invent a likelihood the metamodel has nowhere to put. And the replacement rule,
"every element names a concrete occasion in the active voice, with an actor",
is contradicted by QS-003, the very scenario the same skill cites as exemplary:
its `Artifact`, `Environment` and `Response Measure` read "the web client's
application bundle in `dist/assets`", "a production build" and "≤ 92 kB gzipped".

Three of the four findings were disprovable against a file the offending document
itself points at. Not one of them needed knowledge from outside the repository.

=== What the four rounds have in common

They are not the through-line. Nothing here was a green signal that checked
nothing; there was no signal at all, because prose has none.

The shape is different and worth naming separately: **a rule stated in one place
and contradicted in another place of the same document.** Each half was
defensible alone. What was missing was the pass that reads them against each
other, and that pass is cheap — it needs no tooling, only a reader who has not
just written the text.

The pattern also has a tell, visible in all four: the rule was written from the
example in front of us. `Category` was in view, "in conversation" was not.
Risks were in view, quality scenarios were not. `Source` was in view, `Artifact`
was not. Generalising from the case at hand produces a rule that is exactly right
for the case at hand.

=== The day boundary, and a term that was not in the backlog after all

Late in the day the owner brought two skills from a colleague, `clock-in` and
`clock-out`, to try here. The pattern is good; the text needed most of a rewrite,
including a template that asks for `Test status: 42/43 passing` — hand-copied
numbers in a file nobody re-runs, which is this project's through-line moved
somewhere it survives longer than a comment.

What made it worth doing beyond the ritual is the split it forced. The diary is
never revised and holds what we learned; a file under `progress/` describes now,
is overwritten wholesale and is deleted when its topic ends. Two artifacts, one
rule to keep them apart: a sentence that fits both goes in the diary, because
that is the one that may not be rewritten later.

Writing the first three progress files found something on the first attempt.
`anchorDate` — the term ADR-022 is named after, the example of the error the
whole decision prevents — is still in `FixedCost.java`, in `FixedCostEntity`, in
`V1__initial_schema.sql` as the column `anchor_date`, and in two documents. Pull
request #67 said the decision "cost no implemented code". That was true of the
terms it listed and false of the term it was built on, and neither of us checked
before merging it four hours ago.

It surfaced because the format asks for the *order* of the work rather than a
list of it. Writing "rename before the check, because a check that enforces
agreed terms needs the terms agreed" is what made someone look at where the term
actually sits today.
